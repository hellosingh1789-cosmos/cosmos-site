OUR NEIGHBORHOOD

The Solar System is not simply a collection of eight planets. It is a vast, evolving system of stars, worlds, debris, ice, magnetic fields, and ancient leftovers from its birth.

4.6 billion years ago

HOW IT ALL
BEGAN

Before there were planets, there was a cloud of gas and dust. Gravity turned that cloud into the architecture we see today.

Our Solar System formed about 4.6 billion years ago from a cold, rotating cloud of gas and dust called the solar nebula. It sounds almost impossibly simple for something so grand — but that's really all it took: a cloud, a disturbance, and gravity doing what gravity always does. The disturbance was probably a shockwave from a nearby star exploding as a supernova, which compressed part of the cloud just enough to tip it over the edge and start collapsing inward.

As the cloud fell inward, most of the material piled up at the centre and got increasingly hot and dense. The rest didn't just fall straight down — because the cloud was already rotating, conservation of angular momentum caused everything to spin faster and flatten out into a huge rotating disk, a bit like pizza dough being spun in the air. At the centre of that disk, the young Sun was building up, accumulating mass until the pressure and temperature finally became high enough for nuclear fusion to ignite. That moment — the moment the Sun switched on — is when our solar system truly began.

Out in the disk, things were messy and chaotic. Tiny dust grains collided and stuck together, building up slowly into pebbles, then rocks, then boulders, then planet-sized bodies over millions of years. The stuff that ended up too close to Jupiter's gravity never got to form a full planet — it became the asteroid belt. The material out beyond the frost line, where temperatures were cold enough for ices to survive, built the giant planets. Everything left over became the comets, moons, dwarf planets, and small bodies that fill the solar system today.

01 · COLLAPSE

The Solar Nebula Falls Inward

Imagine a vast, cold cloud of gas and dust just floating in space — that's where our entire solar system came from. Something disturbed it, possibly a nearby star exploding, and gravity did the rest, pulling everything inward. As it collapsed, it started spinning faster and faster, like a figure skater pulling in their arms, and flattened into a huge rotating disk. Everything you see in the solar system today — the Sun, the planets, the moons — all came from that one cloud.

02 · IGNITION

The Sun Is Born

As more and more material piled up at the centre of the disk, it got incredibly hot and dense — we're talking temperatures of millions of degrees. At that point, hydrogen atoms started fusing together into helium, and the Sun switched on for the first time. That same process is still happening right now, every single second, keeping our star burning. Without it, there'd be no light, no warmth, and no life on Earth.

03 · ACCRETION

Worlds Assemble

Out in the disk surrounding the young Sun, particles started bumping into each other and sticking together — dust to pebbles, pebbles to rocks, rocks to boulders. Over millions of years, gravity took over and those boulders grew into the planets we know today. Rocky planets like Earth formed closer to the Sun where it was too hot for ices, while the gas giants grew much further out where they could hold onto huge amounts of material. It's a slow, messy process, but it's how every world in our solar system was built.

04 · CLEARING

A System Takes Shape

Once the planets were established, the solar system looked a lot messier than it does today — there was still tonnes of leftover debris flying around everywhere. The young Sun blasted out powerful winds that swept a lot of the remaining gas and dust away. Jupiter and Saturn, being so massive, flung countless smaller objects out of the solar system entirely or into the distant Oort Cloud. What was left behind became the asteroid belt, the comets, and the other small bodies we still see today.

05 · BOMBARDMENT

A Universe of Collisions

For the first 700 million years or so, the inner planets took an absolute beating from asteroids and comets — scientists call this the Late Heavy Bombardment. The impacts were so intense they melted planetary surfaces and constantly reset the geological clock. Here's the surprising part though: many of those incoming comets were carrying water and organic molecules, and some scientists think that's actually where a lot of Earth's oceans came from. So the same chaos that made early Earth almost uninhabitable may have also delivered the ingredients that eventually made life possible.

06 · STABILITY

The Solar System Settles Down

After about a billion years of mayhem, things finally calmed down and the solar system settled into the familiar arrangement we see today. The planets locked into stable orbits, the bombardment faded, and the Sun settled into a steady, long-lasting phase of hydrogen burning. That stability turned out to be everything — life on Earth needed billions of uninterrupted years to go from simple chemistry to complex organisms. The solar system you're living in right now is the result of 4.6 billion years of gradual order emerging from chaos.

The star at the center

MEET
THE SUN

The Sun contains nearly all of the Solar System's mass and supplies the energy that drives Earth's climate, weather, and life.

Our Sun is a G-type main-sequence star, commonly called a yellow dwarf.

The Sun is a genuinely staggering object. It's a sphere of superheated plasma about 1.39 million kilometres across — wide enough to fit 109 Earths side by side across its diameter, or about 1.3 million Earths packed inside its volume. It contains 99.86% of all the mass in the entire solar system, which means everything else — all eight planets, every moon, every asteroid, every comet — is just the leftover 0.14%. In every meaningful sense, the solar system is the Sun, with a little bit of debris around it.

What keeps the Sun shining is nuclear fusion happening in its core. Every second, the Sun converts around 600 million tonnes of hydrogen into helium, releasing an enormous amount of energy in the process. Here's something that might surprise you: a photon of light created in the Sun's core takes around 100,000 years to work its way out through the dense plasma to the surface — and then just 8 minutes to travel all the way to Earth. The sunlight warming your face right now started its journey before modern humans even existed.

The Sun isn't a static, unchanging ball of fire either. It has an 11-year activity cycle during which sunspots, solar flares, and coronal mass ejections come and go. It constantly streams charged particles outward as the solar wind, which shapes planetary atmospheres, causes auroras on Earth, and extends all the way to the edge of the solar system. The Sun is dynamic, active, and directly connected to everything that happens on every planet in its orbit.

Here's something worth sitting with: the Sun is about halfway through its life. It's been burning for around 4.6 billion years and has roughly another 5 billion to go before it exhausts its hydrogen fuel. When that happens, it'll swell into a red giant, expanding outward to perhaps 200 times its current size — likely engulfing Mercury, Venus, and possibly Earth in the process. Eventually it'll shed its outer layers as a beautiful planetary nebula and leave behind a cooling white dwarf. That future feels impossibly distant, but it's a reminder that the Sun, like everything else in the universe, has a beginning, a middle, and an end.

Between Mars & Jupiter

THE ASTEROID
BELT

A vast population of rocky leftovers from the Solar System's formation—far more interesting than a simple ring of rocks.

The asteroid belt sits in the gap between Mars and Jupiter, roughly 2 to 3.5 astronomical units from the Sun, and it's one of the most misunderstood regions of the solar system. If you've seen it in films — a dense, chaotic field of tumbling rocks that spacecraft have to dodge through — forget that image entirely. The asteroid belt is almost unimaginably empty. If you added up all the material in it, you'd get something less than 4% of the mass of our Moon. The average distance between asteroids is hundreds of thousands of kilometres. Every spacecraft we've ever sent through it — and we've sent many — has passed through without hitting a single thing.

So why is the asteroid belt there at all? The simple answer is Jupiter. In the early solar system, the region between Mars and Jupiter had plenty of material that could have assembled into a planet — but Jupiter's enormous gravitational influence kept stirring that material up, preventing it from settling down and clumping together. The result is a region that's been frozen in a kind of gravitational limbo for 4.6 billion years, full of primitive rocky and metallic bodies that are essentially time capsules from the solar system's birth. Scientists love studying asteroid samples for exactly this reason — they're reading the original ingredients of the planets.

The largest object in the belt is Ceres, which is also classified as a dwarf planet. It's about 940 kilometres across and has surprised scientists enormously. Dawn spacecraft, which orbited Ceres from 2015 to 2018, found bright spots of sodium carbonate inside Occator Crater that appear to be the remnants of a briny liquid that once welled up from the interior. Ceres may have had — and possibly still has — liquid water beneath its surface. That makes the asteroid belt not just a collection of old rocks, but a place that may once have hosted the conditions for chemistry relevant to life.

Asteroids are also increasingly interesting from a practical perspective. Some contain enormous concentrations of metals — iron, nickel, platinum, and other valuable elements that are rare on Earth's surface. A single metallic asteroid a kilometre across could contain more iron and nickel than humanity has ever mined in its entire history. Beyond resources, some asteroids cross Earth's orbit, and tracking them is a serious scientific priority. NASA's DART mission in 2022 proved for the first time that we can actually change an asteroid's orbit by hitting it with a spacecraft — a genuine planetary defence capability that humanity didn't have before.

Beyond Neptune

THE OUTER
FRONTIER

The Solar System doesn't suddenly end after Neptune. Beyond it lies a huge population of icy bodies and ancient material.

Beyond Neptune, the solar system enters a completely different realm. Temperatures out here drop to around -220°C or colder, cold enough that water, methane, and ammonia freeze solid and stay that way. These distant regions are largely untouched since the solar system formed — the material here was never swept up into a planet, never heated by a star, never processed by geology. That makes the outer solar system one of the most scientifically valuable places we can study, a natural deep freeze preserving the original chemistry of the early solar system almost perfectly intact.

The Kuiper Belt

Picture a wide, doughnut-shaped ring of icy debris stretching from roughly 30 to 50 astronomical units from the Sun — that's the Kuiper Belt, and it's enormous. It contains hundreds of thousands of objects larger than 100 kilometres across, along with countless smaller ones, all frozen relics left over from the solar system's formation. Think of it as the solar system's attic: full of old, preserved material that never got swept up into a planet. Because temperatures out here hover around -220°C, those ices have barely changed in 4.6 billion years, making Kuiper Belt objects some of the most scientifically valuable targets we can study.

You've almost certainly heard of the Kuiper Belt's most famous resident — Pluto. For 76 years Pluto was considered the ninth planet, until astronomers started finding more and more similarly-sized worlds out in this region and realised they needed a new category to describe them. The Kuiper Belt is also where short-period comets come from: objects occasionally get nudged inward by gravitational interactions with Neptune, and they fall toward the inner solar system as the comets we see from Earth. NASA's New Horizons spacecraft flew through the Kuiper Belt in 2015, giving us our first close-up look at Pluto and revealing a world far more geologically active and visually stunning than anyone expected.

Dwarf Planets

So what exactly is a dwarf planet? Here's the key distinction: a full planet has to have cleared its orbital neighbourhood of other debris — essentially gravitationally dominating its region of space. Dwarf planets are round worlds that haven't managed that. They're big enough for their own gravity to pull them into a roughly spherical shape, but they share their orbital zone with plenty of other objects. The International Astronomical Union officially recognised five dwarf planets in 2006: Pluto, Eris, Haumea, Makemake, and Ceres — though many astronomers think there are likely dozens more waiting to be formally classified in the outer solar system.

Each of the known dwarf planets is fascinating in its own right. Pluto has a heart-shaped plain of nitrogen ice the size of Texas, towering water-ice mountains, and possibly a liquid ocean buried beneath its frozen surface. Eris is slightly more massive than Pluto despite being smaller — it's so reflective it looks almost like a mirror in space. Haumea spins so fast that it's been squashed into the shape of an egg, completing one rotation every four hours. Makemake has a tiny dark moon that was only discovered in 2016. And Ceres, the only dwarf planet in the inner solar system sitting in the asteroid belt, has bright spots of salt deposits in its craters that suggest water once flowed through its interior. These aren't boring leftovers — they're genuinely complex worlds we've barely begun to explore.

Theoretical distant reservoir

THE OORT
CLOUD

If the Kuiper Belt is the Solar System's icy outskirts, the Oort Cloud may be its enormous spherical frontier.

Illustration showing the Kuiper Belt and the much more distant Oort Cloud in context. The Oort Cloud has not been directly observed; this is a scientific illustration of the region scientists think may surround the Solar System. Credit: ESA · ESA source

Here's something remarkable: we've never directly seen the Oort Cloud, and we may never be able to. It's simply too far away and too diffuse to observe as a structure. What we do have is indirect evidence — specifically, the long-period comets that occasionally fall into the inner solar system from almost every direction. Those comets have orbital paths that trace back to a vast, roughly spherical shell of icy objects surrounding the solar system at distances of perhaps 2,000 to 100,000 astronomical units from the Sun. That shell is the Oort Cloud, and it's the best explanation we have for where those comets originate.

The Oort Cloud is thought to be a remnant of the protoplanetary disk — material that got gravitationally scattered to enormous distances by the giant planets during the early solar system's chaotic youth. At distances of tens of thousands of AU, the Sun's gravitational grip is incredibly faint, meaning these objects are only loosely bound to the solar system. A passing star, a giant molecular cloud, or even the gentle gravitational tides of the Milky Way itself can nudge Oort Cloud objects and send them falling inward toward the Sun — which is exactly what we see when a new long-period comet arrives in our skies. In a very real sense, those spectacular comets are messengers from the outermost edge of our solar neighbourhood.

The most famous long-period comet of all — Comet Halley — completes an orbit roughly every 75 to 76 years and is thought to originate from the Oort Cloud or the outer Kuiper Belt. When comets fall inward from the Oort Cloud, solar heat vaporises their ices and creates the glowing coma and tail that makes them so spectacular in our night sky. Some of these comets are visiting the inner solar system for the very first time — they've been sitting undisturbed out in the cold since the solar system formed, and a random gravitational nudge sent them on a journey inward that will take thousands of years. When you watch a comet cross the sky, you're watching ancient, unprocessed material from the dawn of the solar system putting on one last show before it either escapes the solar system forever or eventually melts away entirely.

Think about the scale.

One astronomical unit is the average Earth–Sun distance. Neptune orbits at about 30 AU. The Oort Cloud, if it exists as predicted, would be vastly farther away—making the planetary region a tiny central neighborhood inside a much larger stellar environment.

Where the Sun's influence reaches

THE
HELIOSPHERE

The Solar System has a boundary shaped not only by gravity, but by a wind of charged particles flowing outward from the Sun.

Every second of every day, the Sun is blowing a stream of charged particles — mostly electrons and protons — outward in all directions at speeds of around 400 to 800 kilometres per second. This is the solar wind, and it doesn't stop at Neptune. It keeps going, pushing outward until it eventually runs into the thin gas of interstellar space. The region carved out by the solar wind — a vast, comet-shaped bubble — is the heliosphere, and it's our solar system's protective shield. It deflects a significant portion of the high-energy cosmic rays coming from the rest of the galaxy, reducing the radiation environment inside the solar system and making it more hospitable for life.

Where the solar wind finally loses the battle against interstellar gas is called the heliopause, and it's the true boundary between our solar system and interstellar space. We know exactly where it is, because two spacecraft have actually crossed it. Voyager 1 crossed the heliopause in 2012 at about 121 AU from the Sun, and Voyager 2 followed in 2018. Both are now travelling through interstellar space, sending back data that scientists never expected to have — direct measurements from beyond the Sun's reach. They're the only human-made objects to have ever left the solar system, and they're still phoning home.

There's something quietly profound about what the heliosphere tells us about our place in the galaxy. We live inside this Sun-blown bubble, protected from the harshest radiation of interstellar space, orbiting comfortably within a star's zone of influence. But the heliosphere is not permanent — its size varies with the solar cycle, and if the Sun were less active, or if we were drifting through a denser region of the interstellar medium, its protective reach would be smaller. The boundary between our solar system and the rest of the galaxy is not a wall — it's a dynamic, breathing frontier, constantly shaped by the balance between our star's output and the environment of the galaxy around it. Voyager 1, now over 23 billion kilometres from Earth and still moving, crossed that frontier and kept going.
